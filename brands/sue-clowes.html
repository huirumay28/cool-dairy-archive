Sue Clowes

Sue Clowes (born 31 October 1957), English textile and fashion designer, Camberwell School of Art; relaunched the label in 2012 with her daughter Marta Melani.

Founder story

After Camberwell School of Art, Clowes printed clothes by hand in a basement flat in Kentish Town on a home-made screen exposer of scrap wood, glass and 150-watt bulbs, burning high-contrast photocopies onto the screens and building colour up with paper stencils (Kerry Taylor Auctions catalogue, from the Sue Clowes Archive). She sold from a stall at Camden Lock; Jon Baker bought her whole stock for Axiom, his early New Romantic shop in the Great Gear Market on the King’s Road; she later had her own shop in Kensington Market (V&A; Paul Gorman).

In 1981 Peter Small turned his Ganton Street shop Street Theatre into The Foundry, a showcase for Clowes’s prints and the window-dressing of the young George O’Dowd (Paul Gorman, The Look). When O’Dowd formed Culture Club, her clothes became the band’s first identity, through the 1982 debut Kissing To Be Clever (V&A; Wikipedia). The idea, in the archive’s words, was that whatever your culture or religion “we are all part of one club called the human race” (Kerry Taylor). The signature was the Tramp or Hobo print: rune-like symbols on Rastafarian-coloured grounds, rolled on with paint rollers and fixed in tumble dryers in her Tabernacle Street studio. Bananarama, Nina Hagen, Toyah Willcox, the Psychedelic Furs and Depeche Mode wore the look too (Kerry Taylor).

Boy George, in his 1995 autobiography Take It Like A Man (quoted in Classic Pop): “I wanted to create clothes that would give Culture Club an identity, like Bow Wow Wow or the Sex Pistols. Sue created brilliant fabric designs based on cultural concepts and a one-nation ideal. We used the Star of David as a symbol which was tied in with Rastafarianism, the lost tribe of Judah. Sue went to the library and looked up Culture Club in Hebrew script and made the first official Culture Club T-shirt… Sue was studious, a real thinker. She wouldn’t throw anything together; it was a painstaking intellectual process.” In 2013 he told the National Post: “The idea was to wear the Utopian dream on our sleeves because we could see it in the band… Sue made all of these smock-ey outfits — they were genderless.” For the 1983 Colour By Numbers era he moved on to Dexter Wong.

In 1987 she moved to Italy and worked on wearable technology with the research team Grado Zero Espace (a Nitinol shape-memory woven fabric; the aerogel-padded Absolute Zero jacket). She relaunched the brand in 2012 with her daughter, and in spring 2023 Supreme released a collection of her early-80s artwork (Destruction of Purity Harrington jacket and shirt, Sex in Heaven ringer tee). Her Culture Club pieces were shown in the V&A’s Club to Catwalk (2013–14) and the Fashion and Textile Museum’s OUTLAWS (2024–25) (Wikipedia).

Style codes

  • Hand screen-printed cotton: smocks, shirts, vests, skirts, headwraps, hand-printed neckties
  • Religious and cross-cultural symbols: Star of David, crosses, Hebrew lettering, runes
  • Tramp / Hobo print on Rastafarian red, gold and green grounds
  • Red roses, St George’s cross, aeroplane silhouettes on white
  • Genderless, loose, layered; worn with headwraps, Hasidic-style hats, stacked socks
