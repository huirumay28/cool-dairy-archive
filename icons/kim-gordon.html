Icon

Kim Gordon

Anti-fashion as a habit, not a pose. She dresses against the generic thing that makes you feel safe.

Born 1953 · 1981– · filed Sunday 16 August 2026

The person

Kim Gordon is the bassist and voice of Sonic Youth, and later a solo artist, writer, and the co-founder of X-Girl. Anti-fashion is a habit, not a pose. The X-Girl clothes were what she and Daisy von Furth already hunted in thrift stores — fitted, clean, a little preppy — because oversized skate menswear did not fit a woman’s body.

She is wary of being sold as a feminist icon. To Numéro (2024): she does not like how feminism is now used as branding. She’d rather not be too self-aware about her image.

The work

Sonic Youth’s Goo (1990) and “Kool Thing” put her on MTV in a cotton singlet, bootcut jeans, black choker. Experimental Jet Set, Trash and No Star (1994) and “Bull in the Heather” brought Kathleen Hanna into the video. The band’s downtown was also a fashion downtown: X-Girl launched 1993–94 with a guerrilla SoHo show two days after Cobain died.

Onstage later she has said she likes to dress against type and wear things that aren’t rock and roll, like a dress. The post-punk dress code was always t-shirts; when Mark Arm asked why she didn’t wear them anymore, she put on a giant T-shirt with nothing, just boots and a choker.

Why the look existed

She dresses against the generic thing that makes you feel safe, and against the rock-uniform she is supposed to wear.

“There’s a certain generic thing that happens with fashion that gives you confidence, like you’re fitting in. And I think there’s always been an element of me that wants to buck against that. I’ve always felt that’s why I’ve never really had such a great sense of style.”

Style.com, 2009; quoted in i-D · 2009

“I was more mining Anita Pallenberg’s style, but also showing how clothes can change you into a different person.”

Chloë Sevigny, Interview

“The post-punk dress code was always t-shirts, and I remember Mark Arm from Mudhoney once asking me why I don’t wear t-shirts anymore. So I started wearing a giant T-shirt with nothing, just boots and a choker.”

Interview, with Chloë Sevigny

“We wanted to do clothes that were fitted and casual. But fitted wasn’t meant to be ‘tight,’ even though sometimes the clothes came out that way. There’s this whole tendency with skater looks that everything needs to be over-sized, but we wanted to do stuff that was more fitted for girls.”

Paper, An Oral History of X-Girl · 2012

What she was reading, hearing, looking at

X-Girl visual language, per the Paper oral history: Joseph Szabo’s 1970s teen photographs; deadstock Army-Navy; the perfect 70s Levi’s 517 cord; “60s meets preppy”; A-line; the least-cool thing made cool. Daisy: it was about taking things that were not punk rock, not indie rock, not hip-hop — and making them that.
